## Why the Fear Existed at All

The fear that the first atomic bomb might ignite Earth's atmosphere was not irrational panic — it was a legitimate scientific question raised by physicists who were doing something never done before: releasing, in a fraction of a second, temperatures at the center of an explosion hotter than the interior of the Sun.

The core of the worry was this: Earth's atmosphere is roughly 78% nitrogen, and nitrogen nuclei can, in principle, fuse with one another (N-14 + N-14 reactions) in a way that releases energy. If the intense heat of a nuclear detonation triggered these fusion reactions throughout the surrounding air, and if the energy released by that fusion heated more air enough to fuse in turn, you would have a self-propagating chain reaction — a "burning" atmosphere that would sweep over the entire planet in moments, killing everything. A similar worry was raised about hydrogen in the oceans. And unlike the bomb's blast, this process would not be limited by geography; it would consume the whole sky, because once started, the reaction would feed on the very air itself.

## Where the Conclusion Actually Came From

The concern traces back to Edward Teller in 1942, during the Berkeley summer study that laid the groundwork for the Manhattan Project. Teller — who had a habit of raising uncomfortable "what ifs" — calculated that a nuclear explosion might be hot enough to start nitrogen fusion in the air. Emil Konopinski checked the calculation and found errors, but the question lingered. In 1943, before any test, Arthur Compton, who directed the Chicago Met Lab, was sufficiently troubled that he had it analyzed. Compton later explained that he had asked: if the odds of igniting the atmosphere were greater than about one in three million, the test could not go forward. The calculations showed the risk was far below even that threshold, so the project proceeded.

The definitive treatment came in the 1946 Los Alamos report LA-602, "Ignition of the Atmosphere with Nuclear Bombs," by Konopinski, Marvin, and Tichenor (with Hans Bethe's earlier work underpinning it). The physics that saved us: a fission bomb simply cannot sustain the temperature long enough, and the fusion reactions lose energy too quickly — hot plasma radiates energy away (via bremsstrahlung) faster than nitrogen fusion can generate it, so the reaction fizzles out rather than spreading. The report even concluded that even if the cross-sections were wrong by a factor of ten, the atmosphere still could not ignite. Later re-examinations in the 1970s, prompted by renewed public worry, reached the same conclusion.

**Short answer:** there was no single moment. The modern picture — outer space as a vast, mostly dark void, with stars and distant galaxies — was assembled gradually.

- **5th century BCE:** Greek atomists such as Leucippus and Democritus speculated about an infinite void beyond the air. This was a minority philosophical idea, not common knowledge.
- **Aristotle and medieval cosmology:** most influential thinkers denied a true void and imagined celestial spheres or an “aether.” The blue daytime sky was generally treated as the appearance of the air/heavens, not as a blue ocean beyond Earth.
- **1640s:** barometers showed that air has weight and that the atmosphere is finite. This was a key step toward “above the air” being a distinct place.
- **17th–19th centuries:** telescopes, balloon flights, and mountain observations showed that the sky darkens with altitude. In **1871**, Rayleigh explained the blue sky as sunlight scattered by air molecules — so without air, the daytime sky would be black.
- **1920s:** Hubble’s work established that many “nebulae” were separate galaxies far outside the Milky Way.
- **1946 onward:** rockets took the first images from near-space; **1961** gave the first direct human observation by Gagarin.

So early people did **not** generally think “outer space is blue like the morning sky.” They saw a blue sky and often explained it with air, aether, or celestial spheres. **Delayed obliteration** means the artery does not necessarily occlude at the moment of injury. Instead, the lumen is progressively narrowed or blocked by one of several overlapping processes.

**1. Traumatic thrombosis.** A roughened or torn intima exposes subendothelial collagen and tissue factor, and platelets adhere, activate, and aggregate there. Fibrin forms and traps red cells, so a thrombus develops. It may start as a mural thrombus, **but** it can propagate or embolize, **so** distal flow can fail hours later.

**2. Subintimal dissection and intimal flap.** A tear allows blood to enter the subintimal plane, **and** the force of flow can lift the distal torn intimal edge. The flap then acts like a valve or curtain, **and** the false lumen can compress the true lumen. The obstruction may be intermittent, **yet** it can progress to complete occlusion.

**3. Intramural hematoma.** Blood can collect within the arterial wall, often from vasa vasorum rupture **or** a small intimal tear. Even though the inner and outer coats remain intact, the hematoma expands under arterial pressure **and** bulges inward, narrowing the lumen.

These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive, **for** a dissection can provoke thrombosis, **and** a hematoma can worsen a dissection. Clinically, this explains why a limb may have a pulse initially **but** later develop ischemia, pain, pallor, paresthesia, or paralysis. Diagnosis is usually by duplex ultrasound or CT angiography, **and** treatment depends on severity; major occlusive trauma often requires surgical or endovascular repair.

## 主题：网络（视频）心理咨询的特殊性、胜任力与伦理规范

## 一、讲者1：网络视频咨询的特点与挑战（研究分享）

**背景**：视频咨询已成为国内普遍的咨询形式，国外研究较多而国内研究较少。讲者团队对此开展了相关研究。

**网络咨询存在的三大问题：**

1. **网络技术问题**
   - 延时、卡顿、掉线等技术困难，需双方花费额外时间准备
   - 突然中断会打乱咨询节奏，转移注意力
   - “草木皆兵”现象：咨询师将正常的、具有治疗意义的沉默误判为网络故障，导致打破咨询设置、过度积极地打破沉默，反而阻碍咨询

2. **咨询技术问题**
   - 缺乏目光接触，难以传递非言语信息（体态、动作、微表情）
   - 需要咨询师有意识地增加共情等技术的表达来弥补

3. **咨询关系问题**
   - 缺乏“在场感”（咨访双方同处一空间的感觉），影响关系建立、共情传递和情感投入

**研究发现**：
- 网络咨询中咨询师更多使用指导性技术、关注认知层面，结构化程度稍高（相对于面对面咨询）
- 面对面咨询中，任务一致性和情感连接共同预测咨询效果；而网络咨询中仅任务一致性起预测作用，即网络咨询更偏任务导向
- 国外研究表明网络咨询更适合短程、结构化强的咨询方式

**结论**：网络咨询对咨询师的共情感受与表达能力、个案概念化能力要求很高，**新手咨询师应慎重选择网络咨询**。

## 二、讲者2：网络咨询的胜任力要求与伦理风险防范

**胜任力要求**：
- 新手咨询师如选择网络咨询，必须配备督导
- 网络空间是非治疗性场所，依据精神卫生法，有诊断的来访者不适合在网络上接待；应挑选适宜对象（如追求个人成长、自我完善的求助者）

**知情同意的特殊内容**（参照伦理守则第八章远程工作部分）：
- 告知地理位置、时差、联系信息
- 说明远程工作的益处、局限和潜在风险
- 来访者有权选择面对面或网络咨询
- 说明技术故障的可能性及处理方案、紧急情况下的应急程序

**风险防范**：
1. **自杀风险防范**：来访者和咨询师均须使用真实姓名（不可匿名），来访者须留下联系人电话、地址，以便危机干预时能找到人
2. **保密风险**：
   - 网络媒介本身可能带来泄密（有研究显示46%的网络咨询师未与来访者讨论保密原则）
   - 视频平台（如Zoom、腾讯）可能被闯入，后台人员可能获取信息
   - 美国要求在加密网络上进行网络咨询；中国目前缺乏此类加密网络，因此更需注意遵守伦理原则

## 三、讲者3：网络团体督导的八项伦理原则

针对现阶段网络团体督导中出现的问题制定，未来将随发展不断修正：

1. **确认特殊性**：区分是在网络做公开课、团体督导还是团体教育
2. **督导师胜任力**：督导师须具备在网络上开展团体督导的胜任力，能应对网络的不确定性
3. **平台选择**：确认网络平台的合法性、资质和可靠性（中国目前缺乏保密性强的专业平台）
4. **确认督导对象**：依据美国咨询师教育督导协会伦理纲要第3.06条，只接受符合条件者（达到训练项目或机构任用条件）作为被督导者
5. **团体规模**：以40人为宜（受屏幕容量限制，或采用20+20双屏配置），需关注整个团体而不仅是报个案者
6. **知情同意**：隐去报告人的可辨识个人信息；避免多任务操作（不同时开会分心）
7. **涉密材料传输**：相对保密的方式为加密Email，密码须通过另外渠道告知
8. **保密承诺**：督导师、主办方及团体成员均须承诺保密，不得将团体督导的二维码、账号、密码用于对外宣传

## 总体结论

网络视频咨询虽具普遍性和一定有效性，但在技术稳定性、非言语信息传递、在场感方面存在固有局限，对咨询师胜任力提出更高要求。从业者需严格遵循知情同意、风险防范和保密等伦理规范，新手咨询师应谨慎选择并在督导支持下开展工作。

**李秋实（高特佳希河资本）**：医药行业**永远有机会**，因为健康是人类终极追求。中老年人消费能力强（如HPV疫苗年销百亿），看好**创新与消费**两大方向：底层医疗技术突破不可阻挡，且约40-50%医药品种属非医保消费领域（OK镜、医美、口腔、二类疫苗等）。

### 3. 创新药估值与风险（张晓峰）

|------|---------|

关于**历史中的当代性**问题：
- 江梅：每个时代的伟大艺术都具有其时代的“当代性”（实验性、前卫性），并终成那个时代的经典。
- 傅军：当代性原指先锋姿态而非单纯时间概念；艺术史是“一浪推一浪”的颠覆与确立的循环；艺术对人类的核心价值在于**思维的拓展**。
- 夏开丰：当代性没有属性，正如主体性的根本是“空无”，正因如此才能不断创造。
- 杨淑清总结：当代性是一种艺术的开放性，不能用任何流派（如抽象性）加以限定。

# 会议记录摘要

## 会议主题
本次论坛围绕**戏剧（尤其是话剧）**展开讨论，参与者为多位演员及戏剧从业者，主要探讨了影视演员转型话剧、话剧表演的特点、戏剧推广、剧场经营以及经典剧目改编等话题。

## 主要讨论内容

### 一、影视演员与话剧表演的差异
- 影视作品可以通过剪辑呈现演员最好的一面，而话剧是即时性、不可剪辑的真实呈现，“谁都骗不过去”。有演员表示，出演话剧是证明自己演技的方式，也是一直以来的梦想和挑战。
- **现场观众的反馈对表演有直接影响**，演员与观众“共同呼吸”，形成独特的剧场氛围和气场，这种体验难以量化，非常奇妙。
- 话剧排练节奏慢（一部戏往往要磨一个月以上），与影视高效的工作方式形成对比，但演员们认为艺术需要慢慢打磨，不应过多考虑功利因素。

- 有与会者提出希望在西北地区（学院路高校、中关村白领聚集区）创办以学生为主的**原创戏剧节**，为有演员梦、导演梦的年轻人提供平台。

- 成功案例：南京大学的剧目从校园起步，最终走向全国。
- 对年轻人的建议：技巧尚不成熟时应**写自己真正有感触的东西**，有感而发的作品才能真正打动观众，不要为了证明“意义”而创作。

- 观众反映票价偏高（如180元），并建议：剧场降低场租、剧团尝试**午夜场**等灵活场次（考虑到北京交通和学生、上班族的时间安排）。

演员们强调话剧的魅力在于**演员在舞台上直接给观众带来快乐，观众又即时反馈到舞台**，这种双向交流是影视无法替代的，希望大家多多支持话剧艺术。

# Button Size and Scale Decisions in the Remote Control Discussion

Based on this kick-off meeting, the team didn't finalize exact button dimensions, but they established several guiding decisions related to size and scale:

## Overall Device Size
- **Palm-sized design**: The Industrial Designer and UI Designer agreed the remote should be small, but with limits — "at least it should hold in your hand properly"
- **No extremes**: The UI Designer explicitly stated they should avoid extremes — "not of this size, not too large... not too small"
- **Reference point**: The remote should not be bigger than an existing standard remote

## Number of Buttons (Scale of the Interface)
- **Minimize buttons**: The team agreed on "not too much buttons" to reduce complexity
- **Three core actions**: The UI Designer suggested the standard actions could be covered by about **three buttons/mental states**:
  - On/off
  - Channel changing
  - Volume adjustment
- **Simple channel navigation**: Most users just go to the next/previous channel rather than direct input (e.g., typing "25"), supporting a simpler button scheme

## Related Design Constraints Affecting Button Design
- **Usability problem identified**: Marketing pointed out that on typical remotes, "people never find a good button in the right place" — they accidentally press off when trying to press something else, indicating a need for **better button placement and differentiation**
- **Handedness consideration**: The Industrial Designer noted that button placement should be **adaptable for left- vs. right-handed users**
- **No touchscreen**: Screens were rejected as "too complex" and "too expensive" given the **€12.50 production cost limit**, meaning physical buttons with good tactile scale would be the approach
- **Child-proofing**: Buttons should be robust and the device unbreakable, since children interact with (and throw/eat) remotes

## Summary
The decision was essentially: **a compact, palm-sized remote with a minimal number of well-placed, well-proportioned buttons** — enough to distinguish core functions clearly so users don't mis-press them, while keeping the overall device small, robust, and within the cost target. Exact button sizing would be worked out in the functional and detailed design phases that followed this meeting.

# Summary: Conceptual Design Meeting Discussion

## Overview
The team held a conceptual design meeting for a remote control, covering component concepts, user interface design, and trend watching. They had 40 minutes to make key decisions.

## Component Concepts (Industrial Designer)
- **Energy:** Chose simple batteries (light nickel or alkaline) over solar cells, since solar cells wouldn't provide enough energy.
- **Material:** Proposed titanium for a premium look, though it was noted that titanium can't support double-curved shapes. Rubber was ruled out due to cost and manufacturing difficulties with button moulding.
- **Interface:** Chose simple push buttons over LCD touch displays to reduce costs, since speech recognition technology would consume at least 5 Euros of the ~12 Euro budget. A non-touch LCD (display-only, for output of information) was kept as a possibility if budget allows.
- **Speech recognition:** Discussed speaker-dependent vs. speaker-independent technology; speaker-independent would be more complex and expensive.

## User Interface Concepts
- Rejected an overly complex "millennium remote control" with many buttons in favor of a simple, low-profile design with few buttons.
- **Colors & customization:** Discussed offering changeable colored panels (like Nokia phones) or color-changing LCD backlights as an optional, paid upgrade (e.g., +2 Euros). Marketing noted some users see their remote as a personal "signature."
- **Smart features:** Proposed voice recognition to lock/unlock the remote (parental control), and the idea of a remote that learns users' preferred channels and volumes — a potential sales pitch ("the remote that knows you").

In this conversation, Professor B had several distinct thoughts about echoes and reverberation, mostly in response to Grad E's report that the re-synthesized audio sometimes contained an "echo of speech in the background":

**1. Why high-pass filtering appeared to reduce reverberation**
He recalled the earlier discussion of Avendano and Hermansky's work, arguing that when Hirsch applied high-pass filtering in the late eighties to reduce reverberation, the benefit may not have come from the filtering itself. Rather, when you filter an all-positive power spectrum you get negative values, and setting those to zero effectively shifts the energy trajectory downward and discards the low-energy portions — and it's precisely in the low-energy parts of speech where reverberation is most audible (reverberation from higher-energy speech shows up there).

**2. The echoes might be fixable by "making the signal nicer"**
He proposed that the re-synthesis processing doesn't have to be restricted to reconstructing the original signal according to some equation — "you also could just try to make it nicer." Specifically, he suggested doing a VAD-like operation and setting very low-energy sections to a near-zero value, so that if these echoes (or pre-echoes) are part of what's hurting recognition, they could simply be removed. He recommended doing this conservatively, so that a mistake is more likely to keep an echo in than to throw out actual speech.

**3. A distinction between two kinds of "reverberation"**
He noted he'd been using the word "reverberation" in two ways: the processing removes some genuine room reverberation, but it also *introduces* reverberation-like artifacts, because averages over multiple windows stretching out up to twelve seconds are subtracted from the speech. As a result, some components can be affected by things that are seconds away, and in low-energy portions you might actually hear "funny things."

**4. The room itself**
When asked about reverberation time in the data, he clarified it was recorded in that very room, guessed the RT was around 0.7–0.8 seconds, and noted they'd never actually measured it.
